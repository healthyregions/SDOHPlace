Many spatial analyses rely on a measure of distance. While straight-line distance (“as the crow flies”) is appropriate for some use-cases, we often want transportation measures that better reflect the barriers between people and places. Transportation has an outsized effect on our health because it shapes how our communities are designed, the opportunities available to us, and environmental exposures like heat and air quality. It is intertwined with many of the other concepts covered in our Research Guides including [Public Transit Equity](https://sdohplace.org/guides/public-transit-equity) and access to critical needs such as [Food](https://sdohplace.org/guides/measuring-food-access-on-a-neighborhood-level), [Greenspace](https://sdohplace.org/guides/greenspace-access), and [Healthcare](https://sdohplace.org/guides/conceptualizing-healthcare-equity.).

For those who want to use transportation measures, this guide provides example applications of transportation measures, discusses the two most common ways mobility data are presented (origin-destination matrices and isochrone), and examines a few common sources for such data. For those hoping to dive deeper, we provide a brief introduction to network analysis, tools and techniques for calculating transportation measures, and nuances of calculating driving, walking, and public transit transportation measures.

## 1. Measuring Spatial Impedance

Many spatial analyses require a measure of *spatial impedance*, a measure of how difficult it is to overcome the space between two things. An everyday example is travel time: traveling to work or the store costs us some time out of our day. Traditionally, spatial impedance – how much space impedes or delays us – has been measured using straight-line distance. Straight-line or *Euclidean* distance tells how long the straight line between two things is, but is a poor measure of spatial impedance for humans because it is “as the crow flies” meaning we ignore buildings, waterways, and other obstructions that would stop us from moving along that straight-line.

In reality, transportation happens along *networks*. Transportation networks are the roads, public transit systems, sidewalks, bike paths, and other systems that get people across space. We refer to them as networks because we model these transportation systems as *nodes* that serve as connection points (e.g., bus stops, intersections, airports) and *edges* that describe how these points are connected (e.g., bus routes, roads, flight paths). By using *network distance*, the distance along these transportation networks, for example how far someone must travel on the sidewalk to reach their destination, we are able to more accurately measure the experience spatial impedance.

While network distance is an improvement on Euclidean distance, we generally prefer to use *network travel time* when measuring spatial impedance for people. Comparing network distance across transportation modes is difficult: 5 kilometers (3.107 miles) is a long walk, but a short drive. However, even within the same mode of transportation, driving 10 miles could take anywhere from 10 minutes on a rural highway to hours in a large city. Using network travel time, a measure of how much time it takes to traverse a transportation network, provides us a meaningful and standardized way to measure spatial impedance. For example, the 15-minute city movement (Moreno et al. 2021; Abbiasov et al. 2024) focuses on ensuring all of our needs are met within a 15-minute zone of walk or bike from our homes.

## 2. Travel Modalities

Now we will briefly address some of the complications that could arise with each of the different kinds of transportation networks. Driving time is often the default because many communities are car-centric, public transit is not always available or reliable, and many resources are not within reasonable distances to bike or walk. The Census can provide data on [car ownership](https://www.census.gov/acs/www/about/why-we-ask-each-question/vehicles/) and use to help determine where that default assumption may fail. Your choice of travel modality may also depend on the specifics of your research questions as well. For example, you may be interested in a certain population that has lower rates of car ownership, or you may be interested in the coverage and shortcomings of your local public transit system.

#### 2.1  Driving

Additional considerations for calculating driving-times include:

* **Congestion and Traffic**: although this data is often very expensive and we usually just assume free-flow travel (everyone moving at the speed limit).
* **Public roads**: OSM has some data on non-traditional roads that fall under their highway tag, but have useful sub-tags to differentiate them like racetracks (track) and service roads (service). Be careful to exclude this data as if you accidentally match an origin or destination to a racetrack, it may not be connected to the rest of your road network!

#### 2.2  Biking and Walking

Additional considerations for calculating walking-times include:

* **Connection**: side-walks and dedicated bike-lanes are not always connected networks due to issues with data or lack of infrastructure. You may need to consider bikers or pedestrians walking along roadways if you want a fully connected transportation network.
* **Elevation and Slope**: changes in elevation can drastically affect the speed and ease of biking and walking.

#### 2.3  Public Transit

Public transit data usually comes in the form of General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) data. This is pretty different from driving, biking, and walking networks as it depends on set public transit stops and schedules that vary across time. For example, your wait for a bus could take anywhere from 1 minute to an hour or more depending on the schedule and when you arrive. Some public transit operations also provide real-time GTFS data that you can compare against their schedules to measure reliability.

A major complication when calculating network travel times on public transit data is that we must consider when we depart and arrive at each bus stop as this affects our waiting time before the next departure. Simply using the transit network without considering the schedule will drastically underestimate travel times. There are algorithms like RAPTOR (Delling et al. 2015) designed to efficiently tackle this problem.

For more information, check out our guide on [Public Transit Equity](https://sdohplace.org/guides/public-transit-equity).

## 3. Working with Travel Time Data

This section goes into a few options for those who want to obtain travel time data. While there are a few open data sources for travel time data, like the OpenTimes site mentioned below, you often need to calculate it yourself to match the specific study area, granularity (e.g., tract vs. county), and timeframe you are working with.  There are two major ways in which travel time data is stored: (1) as origin-destination (O-D) matrices sometimes called travel cost matrices (TCM) and (2) as isochrones that are shapes showing the area within a certain distance. We will discuss a few options for each type of data source.

#### 3.1 Origin-Destination/Travel Cost Matrices

An origin-destination or travel cost matrix is a table that gives the travel costs (most often in time) between origins and destinations. For example, in the table below we can see the “Origin” column with county FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards) codes, a “Destination” column with county FIPS codes, and a “Travel Cost (in minutes)” column. So the first row says that going from county 48113 (Dallas County, TX) to 48113 (still Dallas County, TX) takes 0 minutes. This makes sense, but keep an eye out as these zeroes can throw a wrench in your analyses, for example when dividing by travel time and your travel time is 0! The second row gives the travel time between 48113 (Dallas County, TX) and 48085 (the neighboring Collin County, TX) as 30.1 minutes. 

| Origin | Destination | Travel Cost (in minutes) |
| ------ | ----------- | ------------------------ |
| 48113  | 48113       | 0.0                      |
| 48113  | 48085       | 30.1                     |
| ...    | ...         | ...                      |
| 48085  | 48085       | 0.0                      |

These matrices can be constructed at any scale – county-to-county, house-to-house, etc. – but are most often calculated for standard census units like counties and tracts to facilitate reusability of the data. However, note that these matrices only give an estimate of travel time, usually the travel time between centroids or population-weighted centroids, meaning they are not providing the full picture. In the above example, Dallas and Collin counties share a border, so it is very possible for someone in Dallas County to live across the street from their doctor in Collin County, a trip which likely doesn’t take a full half-hour.

## 5. Summary

We have discussed the basics of transportation network travel time calculation, with a few approaches to obtaining the data including open sources, paid products, and programming packages. While this guide provides the basics of calculating network travel times, there are many additional considerations depending on your study area and goals. If you are working on very large-scale data (e.g., the size of the U.S.) you may need to rely on either cloud computing (Saxon and Snow 2020) or high-performance supercomputers (Michels et al. 2024). Depending on your task, you may also want to include more personal considerations like cost (Pereira et al. 2024), environmental exposure (Liu et al. 2025), or perceptions of safety (Kar et al. 2024). Some of these additional considerations and applications of network travel time are covered in further SDOH Guides, so be sure to check our additional resources!
