## Introduction

The natural environment shapes the conditions of daily life in ways inseparable from the social and built contexts surrounding it. Robert Bullard (2000) wrote that all people have a basic right to “live, work, play, go to school, and worship in a clean and healthy environment.” Understood this way, the natural environment is the air, water, ecological conditions, and climate where daily life unfolds. What the natural environment means and how communities relate to it vary considerably and are shaped as much by history and culture as by what is physically present. Across those varied contexts, natural environmental conditions have well-documented consequences for cardiovascular health, mental wellbeing, respiratory disease, developmental outcomes, and mortality (Marshall et al, 2026; Sayyed et al, 2024; Zheng et al, 2026). The strength of the evidence varies by dimension, with air quality associations among the most established and biodiversity and blue space associations promising but still developing (Gascon et al, 2017; Marshall et al, 2026; Shanahan et al, 2015). The conditions tend to concentrate and compound: communities facing elevated exposure to pollution often also have the least access to safe water and restorative natural spaces, producing cumulative burdens (Morello-Frosch et al, 2011). Those same communities are also usually navigating broader social and economic disadvantage – a pattern that reflects decades of decisions about where to invest, where to site hazards, and whose neighborhoods to protect (Hoffman et al, 2020; Motairek et al, 2023). 

Navigating the natural environment as a social determinant of health (SDOH) requires working across a landscape of indices, platforms, datasets, and methods built for different purposes – conservation, ecology, environmental science – that do not always translate cleanly to health and equity research. Measuring it is complicated by the breadth of what counts as the natural environment, the difficulty of finding data precise enough to be meaningful at the community scale, and the gap between what a measure captures and what communities experience. This orients researchers to the measures available for studying the natural environment as a social determinant of health, and how to use them in ways that keep equity at the forefront.

This guide focuses on five key dimensions of the natural environment at the community level: blue space and water quality, biodiversity and ecological quality, air quality, heat and land surface temperature, and relational and perception measures that span across all dimensions. These are among the most studied in health and SDOH research, but the natural environment is broad, and the measures to capture it are uneven. Vegetation and greenspace are covered in a companion guide and appear here only where they show up in cross-cutting tools. The summary table in this guide consolidates measures across dimensions. Readers can navigate directly to the dimensions most relevant to their work or read the full guide. The measures vary in what they require and reveal, and the most useful analyses tend to combine approaches rather than rely on a single measure. 

## Measuring the natural environment:

#### *Blue space and water quality*

Blue space – rivers, lakes, wetlands, coastlines, and other environments where water is central – has emerging associations with mental health and physical activity, with stronger evidence for coastal than inland waters, where there search is thinner and findings less consistent (Gascon et al, 2017; Völker& Kistemann, 2011; Wang et al, 2025). For many communities, the nearest blue space is a river, stream, or lake, which is where that thinner evidence sits. Measuring blue space at the community scale means establishing where water is, how close communities are to it, and what conditions it is in, which often requires different datasets. Inland and coastal waters differ enough physically that the same measure does not always serve both equally well:coastlines are fixed and continuous, while rivers and smaller lakes shift seasonally and can fall below the resolution of coarser data (Gascon, 2017). Land cover data identifies surface water without additional processing;satellite-derived indices offer a more precise alternative that can detect smaller, more dynamic bodies that coarser data misses. Wetlands deserve particular attention as a distinct blue space type often overlooked when research focuses on rivers, lakes, and coasts because they differ ecologically from open water, and provide distinct environmental services (Wang et al,2025). 

Access is usually measured as proximity, typically as either straight-line or network distance. Straight-line(Euclidean) distance measures the direct distance between two points, while network distance follows the routes people actually travel; the SDOH & Health Place Community Toolkit offers this distinction in its [Research Design & Analysis](https://toolkit.sdohplace.org/05-analysis.html#calculating-spatial-variables) section. The difference is significant for blue space because physical barriers such as highways, private land, and fenced industrial waterways frequently lie between communities and water that appears nearby on a map (Labib et al, 2020). 

Alongside presence and access, federal and state databases record which water bodies fail to meet quality standards for recreation, aquatic life, or drinking water supply. Some research has linked surface water quality to population health outcomes; however, this connection is not yet well established as an SDOH measure (Wheeler et al, 2015). These measures don’t capture what water means to a community. Its cultural and spiritual significance, and its role in subsistence, memory, and place identity, make it one of the most consequential dimensions of the natural environment for many communities. 

Drinking water quality is a related and consequential SDOH dimension, but it reflects the treatment and delivery systems between a source and a tap rather than the condition of water in place.

#### *Biodiversity and ecological quality*

Biodiversity encompasses the variety of species, habitats, and ecological processes in a given area, which differs from the amount of vegetation present. Evidence linking biodiversity to health and well being is growing. It appears particularly meaningful in urban areas and more deprived communities, though the evidence base is thinner and less consistent than for other dimensions in this guide (Shanahan et al, 2015; Spotswood et al, 2025; Wheeler et al, 2015;Wu et al, 2024). The central measurement challenge for biodiversity and ecological quality is that the data researchers would ideally use are thinner in the communities where deficits are most pronounced (Beninde et al, 2023;Shanahan et al, 2015). 

Most approaches draw on volunteer-contributed species observation platforms, where naturalists log what they see and where. Researchers collect this data for ecological rather than health and equity purposes, which shapes what is available and how it can be used (Beninde et al, 2023; Tiago et al, 2024). Aggregating this data to the neighborhood scale estimates how many species are present in a given environment. Tree and plant species diversity is distinct from overall tree canopy cover – covered in the companion [greenspace guide](https://sdohplace.org/guides/greenspace-access) – because ecological variety in the urban tree canopy has its own health associations and is measurable through urban forestry databases in some cities(Shanahan et al, 2015). Where observation is sparse, land cover classification data offer a nationally consistent alternative that can approximate habitat diversity. 

Protected and designated area data serve as indicators of ecological quality and have shown associations with population health in some research, particularly in more deprived urban communities (Wheeler et al, 2015). Although community-level tools are not yet established, acoustic complexity, such as birdsong or wind through vegetation, is beginning to be measured in health research (Wu et al,2024). Biodiversity is not uniformly health-positive; vector-borne habitats,allergen loads, and pollen are real health concerns alongside the benefits(Shanahan et al, 2015; Wheeler et al, 2015). 

#### *Air quality*

Air quality research has the most established evidence base of any natural environment dimension, with well-documented links to cardiovascular and respiratory disease, mortality, and adverse birth outcomes (Marshall et al, 2026; Sayyed etal, 2024). Fine particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide are the most studied pollutants, though ozone, wildfire smoke, and other pollutants are increasingly relevant to community-level health research (Marshall et al, 2026). Most official monitoring networks are built for regulatory compliance rather than equity analysis, and working around their limits can require more complex approaches (Marshall et al, 2026; Osakwe et al, 2024).

Ground-based monitors are the most accessible starting point, and are publicly available,well-validated, and tied to established health standards. Monitors are sparse in rural and low-income areas, and evidence suggests that placement underestimates exposure in communities of color (Marshall et al, 2026).Satellite data closes much of that distance, covers nearly the entire country,and shows a consistent pattern of communities of color and lower-income communities facing higher pollution levels across measurement approaches(Marshall et al, 2026; Motairek et al, 2023; Tessum et al, 2021). Finer-scale computational models push resolution to the block level in some cases,capturing the within-neighborhood variation that matters most for equity analysis, though at substantially higher technical cost (Marshall et al, 2026).

Wildfire smoke remains an increasingly significant gap in air quality measurement. Standard products capture it imperfectly, and because it is episodic, annual averages miss the acute exposure events that drive health impacts (Marshall et al, 2026). These burdens generally fall disproportionately on communities with less capacity to shelter indoors or evacuate (Marshall et al, 2026). Low-cost community sensor networks are emerging options that fill many of these gaps and put air quality data directly into the hands of communities (Karasaki et al, 2026; Marshall et al,2026). 

#### *Heat and land surface temperature*

Extreme heat is among the most direct ways the natural environment affects health.Heat-related illness and mortality fall disproportionately on elderly residents, outdoor workers, and people in older housing without cooling(Hoffman et al, 2020; Zheng et al, 2026). A community’s heat exposure depends heavily on local conditions such as tree canopy, building density, surface materials, and housing age (Hoffman et al, 2020; Zheng et al, 2026). Climate change is intensifying both the frequency and severity of extreme heat events,making this an increasingly urgent dimension of natural environment measurement at community-scale (Hoffman et al, 2020; Zheng et al, 2026). 

Satellite-derived surface temperature is the standard first measure for urban heat. It is freely available at a resolution suitable for neighborhood-level analysis and isr ecorded over a long enough span to track change over time (Hoffman et al,2020; Sayyed et al, 2024). Land surface temperature captures the spatial pattern of heat across a community well, but measures surface conditions rather than the air temperature people breathe and feel. Crucially, nighttime temperatures are as consequential for health as daytime peaks. Land surfaces and buildings absorb heat during the day and release it at night, which is why hot nights in dense urban areas are particularly dangerous, and why the time of day a satellite passes overhead affects what it captures (Hoffman et al, 2020;Zheng et al, 2026). 

Some cities and researchers have developed heat vulnerability indices that combine surface temperatures with social vulnerability indicators such as housing age,cooling access, and demographic characteristics, which better reflect experienced heat burden than surface temperatures (Zheng et al, 2026). Ambient air temperature monitoring networks exist but are sparse in most cities(Karasaki et al, 2026). 

#### *Relational and perception measures*

Each dimension covered in this guide has relational dimensions of environmental experience specific to it. This section pulls together the methodological approaches that cut across those dimensions to situate the ways researchers collect data on how communities perceive, use, and move through the natural environment. Surveys and interviews are the most established methods, asking residents directly about how they perceive and relate to the natural environment around them; where sample sizes allow, responses can be aggregated to the community level (Jelks et al, 2018; Dominguez-Rendon et al, 2024). Trained observer assessment uses structured protocols to evaluate environmental conditions at the street and neighborhood scales, capturing quality, usability,and safety as people experience them; street-view imagery analysis offers a lower-cost alternative that extends across larger areas (Karasaki et al, 2026).

Community-generated mapping and methods like photo voice and walking interviews are participatory,community-engaged approaches that produce spatial and narrative records of environmental experience in place (Jelks et al, 2018). Social media data can detect environmental concern at scale through geolocated platform data.However, the communities bearing the most severe burden tend to produce the weakest signal, which limits its value as a standalone tool (Knoble & Yu,2023). 

Relationships to water, land, and ecological conditions vary widely across cultural contexts,histories, and lived experiences. Perceptions of safety and restoration, the ecological knowledge communities hold, and what counts as meaningful access are all shaped by structural conditions, and relational methods surface them onlywhen deliberately designed to do so (Song & Kwan, 2023). Nonetheless, used alongside material measures, relational approaches add important context, and a combination tends to produce a more complete and equitable account of environmental conditions (Jelks et al, 2018; Morello-Frosch et al, 2011).
